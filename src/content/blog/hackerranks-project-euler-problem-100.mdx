<figure><img alt="" src="/medium/hackerranks-project-euler-problem-100/01-1-b8rtzjqcjrocapp2wj3egw.png" /></figure><p>This article documents the grueling two weeks I spent on <a href="https://www.hackerrank.com/contests/projecteuler/challenges/euler100/problem">HackerRank’s version of Project Euler Problem #100</a>. Over the course of those two weeks, I had a crash course in Pell’s Equations, Chakravala method, Diophantine Solvers, Quadratic Congruences, Chinese Remainder Theorem, Hensel’s Lemma, the Tonelli-Shanks algorithm, implementing ValueErrors, and building testing suites for my code. If the leaderboard is to be believed, less than 10% of people complete the attempt, and I am only the 60th account to do it! I won’t give too much away, it’s hard enough to implement even with hints, but I hope that this page can at least guide some people on the right path.</p>
<p>The <a href="https://projecteuler.net/problem=100">original problem</a> is relatively quite simple; re-using what prior problems taught you about convergences should enable quick work using continued fraction sequences to find that one answer.</p>
<figure><img alt="" src="/medium/hackerranks-project-euler-problem-100/02-1-y0gcgfhs0b3nm2aqqi4eqa.png" /><figcaption>projecteuler.com</figcaption></figure><p>But HackerRank always ups the complexity; instead of finding the answer that equals 1/2, they want you to find the solution for ratio P/Q, with 1 ≤P &lt; Q ≤ 10⁷, and find <em>that</em> probability for numbers up to 10¹⁵! Potentially 1000 times!</p>
<figure><img alt="" src="/medium/hackerranks-project-euler-problem-100/03-1-vmwlszk2ece7btropuzu_g.png" /><figcaption>HackerRank’s Constraints</figcaption></figure><p>This is a <em>significant</em> upgrade from the original problem that now <em>generalizes </em>the problem of continued fractions, convergences, congruencies, and Diophantine equations. It says every answer in the output will be less than 2⁶³, but that is small comfort because by the end, it’s clear that there are <em>intermediate results</em> that are over 2⁶³, so you’re going to have to account for that! I even made an isSquare() function without non-integer operations, though that later turned out to be overkill.</p>
<p>My <em>first</em> attempt was a big long dead end! Having dealt with Pell’s type equations in the past, I was lulled into false confidence by thinking I could just rearrange the equation into a Pell’s like formula and find the solution, then use convergences to scale the answers.</p>
<p>The original problem starts like this, if B = Blue Disks and T = Total Disks:</p>
<figure><img alt="" src="/medium/hackerranks-project-euler-problem-100/04-0-k-wx6wems2ca9nrj.png" /></figure><p>Algebraically, I spent a lot of time turning it into other messes, always trying to avoid leaving fractions because I knew it had to be integer solutions.</p>
<p>Eventually, I landed on this:<strong>(2QB − Q)² − QP(2T − 1)² = Q(Q − P)</strong></p>
<p>Ah, it <em>kinda</em> looks like a Pell’s equation, right? x² -Dy² = N? Maybe all I have to do is find the generalized version for solving for N, then reverse out what x and y are!</p>
<p>What followed was long and fruitless and with many pitfalls; maybe there’s a way to solve it like this, but I did not find it! Equating x and y to those forms meant also checking for divisibility, and there were some solutions to this equation that were always even, etc. I learned about Chakravala’s Method for solving Pell’s equations, which seemed more elegant than the continued fraction method, but ultimately it wasn’t helpful. I didn’t understand how to manage the edge cases; I almost went down a path of graphing every combination of evens and odds to find a discrete way of knowing whether or not there would be a solution. But ultimately, I gave up on that line of questioning and went back to the original. What if I was thinking about it the wrong way?</p>
<p>So, I returned to the original equation and went for simplicity. What if I just expanded the terms and worked with that?</p>
<p><strong>QB² − PT² − QB + PT = 0</strong></p>
<p>Only problem was, I didn’t know how to deal with this more general quadratic congruence. But, after a heap of healthy googling, I came across <a href="https://www.alpertron.com.ar/ENGLISH.HTM">Dario Alpern’s incredible site</a>, where he has a calculator that <em>explains every step.</em> It was a treasure trove of information, but it wasn’t quite enough for me to begin understanding how it even worked, or why.</p>
<p>He <em>was</em> responsive via email, but I also didn’t want anybody to spoonfeed me what was going on, so I set about the arduous task of reverse-engineering the calculator to the best of my abilities, which brings me to another source you will find googling anything quadratic number theory, <a href="http://www.numbertheory.org/keith.html">Keith Matthews</a>, a mathematician who has published many papers about number theory. In one paper in particular he describes <a href="https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?repid=rep1&amp;type=pdf&amp;doi=ab576e7bfc9c90800d306aa3b3685b34c8de7523">the solubility and solutions for equations of the type Ax² + Bx + C = N</a>, and in there I found a parallel with what Dario was doing on his site. I won’t admit to fully understanding all the whys, but at least I could see what they were both doing in common.</p>
<p>In broad strokes, Dario’s calculator used various transformations to turn this:</p>
<p><strong>Ax² + Bxy + Cy² +Dx +Ey +F = 0</strong></p>
<p>…into a new equation of the form <strong>aU² + bUV +cV² = N.</strong></p>
<p>It involves “Legendre” transformations, as well as one that changes whether <strong>a </strong>and <strong>N</strong> are coprime. After all that,<em> </em>I’ll try to explain succinctly without the language of number theorists that I don’t understand.</p>
<p>You have to try to solve all equations of the form<strong> aU² + bUV + cV² ≡ 0 mod N/p²</strong></p>
<p>Where <strong>p² </strong>is all the squares which N is divisible by. And solving just <em>one</em> of those equations involves factoring the right-hand side, finding the solutions to those congruences, then recombining them with the Chinese Remainder Theorem.</p>
<p>I first implemented a crude, brute-force quadratic congruence solver. Instead of paying attention to heuristics, I just wanted to get things running. But then, I realized by naive search, this congruence solver was taking far too much time. I had to learn how to solve quadratic congruences <em>in general.</em></p>
<p>After some more searching, I had to discover modular square roots, which led to a deep rabbit hole. This StackExchange showed me how to use the quadratic equation to solve quadratic congruences generally, but…</p>
<p><a href="https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/4467793/can-we-generalize-the-quadratic-formula-to-modular-arithmetic">Can we generalize the quadratic formula to modular arithmetic?</a></p>
<p>Luckily, I’d already implemented modular inverses from the Chinese Remainder Theorem, so now all I had to do was implement <em>modular square rooting</em>, which is <em>much </em>more complicated than I’d thought. Basically, taking modular square roots involves a similar exercise in factoring the modulus. And the square roots depend on the value of the modulus; for <em>half</em> of the odd primes there are algorithms like Tonelli-Shanks, but for the only non-odd prime, 2, I was scratching my head. But, then coming across Hensel’s Lemma:</p>
<p><a href="https://brilliant.org/wiki/hensels-lemma/">Hensel's Lemma | Brilliant Math &amp; Science Wiki</a></p>
<p>I started to understand that I could solve for simple prime modulus, and then “lift” those solutions to higher powers. This was what I needed for my algorithm.</p>
<p>After slowly and surely implementing that and running it against a battery of randomized testing, I ended up answering my own StackExchange question with an algorithm I understood after poring through the dense jargon of various searches:</p>
<p><a href="https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/4747171/modular-square-root-function-with-hensels-stronger-lemma-algorithm-for-bmod">Modular square root function with Hensel's Stronger Lemma; algorithm for $\bmod 2^x$? Also $x^2 = a\pmod&#123;p^n&#125;$ when $\gcd(a,p) \neq $1</a></p>
<p>So, now, I was directly and quickly solving the congruences!</p>
<p>But what to even do with those solutions? It turns out, there’s another algorithm required at this stage. Those solutions are part of a continued fraction expansion of the form:</p>
<figure><img alt="" src="/medium/hackerranks-project-euler-problem-100/05-0-kxmnlzujjlmd9mec.png" /></figure><p>…whose convergences of form <strong>(V/-k)</strong> are used to solve an equation: <strong>PV² + QVk + Rk² = 1. </strong>P,Q,R are described on Dario’s site. I haven’t been able to find other sources on his methods, unfortunately, so let’s hope it doesn’t go offline!</p>
<p>Head spinning yet?</p>
<p>After scaling my squareroot continued fraction function into a more generalized continued fraction function, I wound up with multiple efficiency issues. For difficult numbers, the continued fraction expansion could be hundreds of thousands of terms long…was I to search all of them?</p>
<p>I tried implementing a yielding convergence generator to get around having to make the continued fraction expansion in its entirety, since I only needed to stop at the first convergence I found that worked! But, the problem was, <em>sometimes there were no solutions. </em>I needed a way to terminate the function, and the only way was to know the period of the continued fraction, and the only way to know that, was to make the entire continued fraction. After bashing my head against a few heuristics that just didn’t work, I ultimately gave in.</p>
<p>A sidenote, I toyed around with a variety of try/catch/exceptions in the course of implementing this. Since there are often so many different cases in which modular inverses, or modular square roots, or lack of solutions were found, I wanted a way to propagate issues all the way up instead of having to pass around a filtering boolean. Perhaps not the most ideal solution, but it worked for me and was a good crash course in how to use exceptions to my advantage.</p>
<p>Finally, I had all the pieces in place. After finding all the solutions to the deepest equations, you have to <em>reverse all the transformations</em> applied to finally get back to options for B and T. But, these would just be the <em>first roots</em> out of an <em>infinite </em>number of roots! You have to implement a generalized quadratic recursive function!</p>
<p><a href="https://www.alpertron.com.ar/METHODS.HTM">Methods to solve quadratic Diophantine equations</a></p>
<p>Also on Dario’s site :)</p>
<p>I was having fun using yield in Python; no longer having to pass in previous variables and iterate, it could keep its own state. Using a factory function, I would generate iterators for each problem. The variables for the recursive functions <em>may </em>rely on other functions you may have already created ;)</p>
<p>I was getting all of the testcases except one for a wrong answer and two for timeout errors. The timeout errors I could understand, but a wrong answer? Was there a bug in this labyrinth of code? I was almost ready to give up if that were the case, but I decided to persist and simply expand my testing suite. Maybe there were edge cases I didn’t understand? A naive solver could show me where my blindspots were, at least for some lower values.</p>
<p>Iterating from Q = 2 and going upwards through every loop, I realized I had assumed away an edge case. From the beginning, based on the original Pell’s equation, I’d assumed PQ could not be square. <em>Most </em>such cases fail. But not <em>all</em> of them. It’s an exercise for the reader, but filtering out <em>exactly</em> why a problem had no solution is close but has more elements than evaluating squareness. I had to rely on very verbose logging, factoring, and pattern recognition through my testing suite to eventually nail down a real function that tested for all the edge cases.</p>
<p>Finally, no more <em>wrong</em> answers, at least! Just time out errors on the last two testcases, 16 and 17. Using cProfile I started to whittle away at little optimizations I thought I’d made but were actually slowing things down. A helper class for factors got deprecated to a regular Counter(). More than one dict() was implemented to keep track of previous values and memo-ize them. List comprehensions, redundant checks, spare seconds were whittled away. But it didn’t feel <em>enough</em>.</p>
<p>Going back to Dario’s site, and perhaps I would never have noticed this without his site, I saw that many, well, <em>most</em> of the searches along quadratic congruences ended in nothing. The right roots were often found in very specific paths. I started seeing a pattern amongst those failures, as well as patterns amongst the roots that would come back from many equations. They all had certain roots in common…! But some solutions did not lie in those common roots…and why? And would factoring help?</p>
<p>Suffice it to say, nailing the final heuristic dropped my testing suite from 10 seconds long to 2 seconds long, and it was the final optimization that let me pass the problem with a score of 100%.</p>
<p>This was certainly the most grueling exercise in HackerRank’s Project Euler I’d come across. Perhaps not necessarily practical in my self-teaching, but a good exercise in <em>many</em> disciplines. From error-catching, to generator functions, to many other articles and pages about number theory and p-adics I cannot understand, I got a thorough overview of the debugging features in VS Code as well as how nice it is to work from a clean, simple IDE. What’s even funnier that I barely remember now, is that I <em>started </em>this problem in C# as an exercise to learn C#! As soon as I saw that I’d need BigNums I switched back to python, as that was a level of tediousness I did <em>not</em> want to deal with while learning C#.</p>
<p>I feel if I wanted to I could continue to tweak until I’d fully developed a functional full quadratic solver; I think it’s 90% there. I never knew that the transformations could simplify the problem so much! But I’m happy to rest on my laurels for now, I did a FIFA victory lap around my living room as soon as I passed. It was a good feeling! Solving a puzzle has never felt so satisfying. I have to thank Dario Alpern for laying the groundwork.</p>
